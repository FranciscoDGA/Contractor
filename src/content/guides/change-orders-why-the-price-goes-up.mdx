**A change order is a written, priced, and signed amendment to a signed contract, and the price goes up afterward whenever the original quote left something undefined — a low allowance, a vague scope line, or time-and-materials billing with no stated rates — because those gaps were priced as extras from the start.**

Every project changes once demolition starts. The difference between a manageable adjustment and a ballooning budget is not whether changes happen, but whether each change arrives on paper with a number attached before anyone picks up a tool.

## What a change order actually is

A change order is a short document: what changed, why it changed, what it costs in labor and materials, how it moves the completion date, and two signatures. Maine's Attorney General states that all change orders to a home construction contract must be in writing and signed by both parties, and Connecticut's home improvement statute requires each change in the terms and conditions to be in writing and signed by owner and contractor. Those are not formalities — they are the mechanism that keeps an agreed price agreed.

> A verbal approval is not a change order. It is a memory of a conversation, and memories disagree about what was said once the invoice arrives.

The unhealthy version of the clause reads "additional work billed at time-and-materials rates" and stops there. That sentence has no ceiling, no rate, and no markup, which is why so many final bills end up above the quote they were built on.

## Why the price goes up

| Driver | How it shows up on the bill | The fix, before signature |
|---|---|---|
| Undefined allowance | "Tile over allowance: $2,400" | State the amount and what it buys |
| Missing scope line | Demo, disposal, or painting billed separately | Write the full scope into the body |
| Open time-and-materials | Hours billed with no rate stated | Stated hourly rate for each trade |
| Unpriced site condition | Repairs found behind a wall | Exclusion list plus a written alert process |
| Material upgrade | Priced at the new cost with a new markup | Markup stated in the contract terms |
| Schedule compression | Overtime added without notice | Notice requirement before premium time |

Each row is a place where the original document was silent, and silence is what gets priced later. ## The four terms to require

Before any extra work is performed, ask for these four terms in the change-order clause:

1. **Written and signed first.** No change order is valid until it is written, priced, and signed by both parties before work proceeds.
2. **Stated rates.** Hourly rates per trade, and a stated markup on materials added, appear in the contract rather than being discovered later.
3. **A not-to-exceed cap.** Undefined time-and-materials work carries a ceiling; when the cap is reached, work stops until you approve more in writing.
4. **A response window.** The clause states how many days you have to respond to a change request, so no work proceeds by default while you are away.

Terms like these are exactly what the [contractor quote template](/guides/contractor-quote-template/) is built to hold, and they belong in the contract rather than in the conversation that preceded it.

## The questions to ask

- "What is your change-order process, and where is it written in this document?"
- "What hourly rate and what material markup apply to anything added?"
- "Is there a not-to-exceed amount for undefined work, and what happens when it is reached?"
- "Which items are allowances, and what does each allowance actually buy?"
- "What is excluded from this price, and how will you alert me before performing excluded work?"
- "Who pulls the permit, and is the fee inside this total?"

Answers that change what you pay belong on the page. The complete list is in [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/).

## When an increase is legitimate

A legitimate change order is one you requested in writing, or one driven by a site condition that could not be seen through a wall — and it arrives priced and signed before the work starts. An unauthorized overage is an amount that appears on a final invoice with no signed change order behind it, or a rate that was never stated anywhere in the contract.

The second case is a dispute, not a negotiation. Start with the contract's change-order clause, ask for the authorization records, and compare the invoice line by line against the original scope — the full procedure is in [what to do when the final bill is higher than the quote](/guides/final-bill-higher-than-the-quote/). For the review sequence that catches these clauses before signature, use [how to review a contractor quote](/guides/how-to-review-a-contractor-quote/).

## The bottom line

Change orders are normal; unpriced ones are optional. Fixed allowances, a written scope, stated rates, a not-to-exceed cap, and signed-before-work language will hold a budget together through a renovation that changes anyway.
