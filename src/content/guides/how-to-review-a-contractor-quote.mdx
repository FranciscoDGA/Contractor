**Review a contractor quote in one fixed sequence — document type, scope, line items, exclusions, allowances, payment schedule, change orders, permits and license, three bids, then the decision — because each check can only be made against the step before it.**

A quote is a stack of claims about money, and the order you read them in decides whether you can test them. Start with the total and you have nothing to measure it against. Work the sequence and each layer becomes evidence for the next: scope justifies the line items, exclusions bound them, and the payment schedule shows when your money is actually at risk.

## Step 1: Confirm what kind of document you are holding

An [estimate, a quote, and a bid](/guides/contractor-estimate-vs-quote-vs-bid/) are three different promises, and only one is a fixed price for a defined scope. Check the header first: contractor name, address, license number, date, version, and how long the number stays valid. A document with no date cannot be matched to a later invoice, because there is no record of which version anyone agreed to.

| Step | What you confirm | What a failure signals |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Document type | Type of document plus a date | You cannot tell what was promised |
| 2. Scope | Rooms, surfaces, systems, prep, cleanup | The job is undefined, so the price is |
| 3. Line items | Labor split from materials | No number can be challenged alone |
| 4. Exclusions | Written list of what is excluded | Omitted work returns as a bill |
| 5. Allowances | Amount and what it assumes | A low placeholder becomes a change order |
| 6. Payment schedule | Deposit, milestones, final payment | Money leaves before work starts |
| 7. Change orders | Written, priced, signed first | Extras are priced with no ceiling |
| 8. Permits and license | Who pulls, who pays, license number | A later fee lands on you |
| 9. Three bids | Scope-normalized comparison | Price gaps stay unexplained |
| 10. Decision | Terms fixed in a contract | Only the conversation survives |

## Steps 2-4: scope, line items, exclusions

Read the scope and compare it with what you actually discussed — rooms, surfaces, systems, demolition, prep, installation, and cleanup rather than a category label. Then the numbers: a readable quote separates labor from materials on major lines, so ask for the split wherever a line is bundled. The method is in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/).

Exclusions come before the total, not after. Permits, disposal, painting after the trades, appliance moves, and repairs found behind walls are normal exclusions, and a long list often signals experience rather than evasion; what matters is that it is in writing. The warning signs are catalogued in [red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/).

## Steps 5-6: allowances and the payment schedule

An allowance is a placeholder budget for items you have not chosen yet: tile, fixtures, cabinets, counters, lighting. It is honest when it states the amount and what that amount buys. It becomes a trap when it is low enough to make the bid look competitive and your real selection later arrives as an extra charge.

> An allowance that buys less than your actual selection is not a discount. It is a scheduled increase, written down before you have noticed it.

The payment schedule is where quotes cause the most damage. Look for a deposit, progress payments tied to named milestones, and a final payment after completion. The FTC's guidance is direct: do not pay the full amount up front, and do not make the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied with it. Some states also cap the down payment, so read [how much a contractor can ask as a deposit](/guides/how-much-can-a-contractor-ask-as-a-deposit/) before comparing schedules.

## Steps 7-8: change orders, permits, and license

Find the change-order clause in the terms. The protective version requires changes to be written, priced, and signed before work proceeds, with stated hourly rates and a stated markup on added materials — see [change orders and why the price goes up](/guides/change-orders-why-the-price-goes-up/). Undefined time-and-materials language with no rates is not a term; it is an open price.

Then the paperwork pass: who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether that fee is inside the total. Whether your project needs a permit or an inspection is a question for your local building authority or a licensed professional. Verify the license yourself with [how to verify a contractor license](/guides/how-to-verify-a-contractor-license/) rather than accepting a card or a photograph.

## Steps 9-10: compare three bids, then decide

The FTC recommends at least three written estimates before you commit, which is where scope differences become visible; [how many contractor quotes you should get](/guides/how-many-contractor-quotes-should-i-get/) explains why three is the floor. Put the bids side by side against one written scope with [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/), check each markup against [what is a fair markup on materials](/guides/what-is-a-fair-markup-on-materials/), and test your number against a published range with the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) and a cost guide such as [is $15,000 too much for a roof replacement](/guides/is-15000-too-much-for-roof-replacement/).

| Response you get | What it tells you | Next move |
|---|---|---|
| Missing term supplied in writing | The gap was clerical | Keep going |
| Explanation with no written term | The term does not exist yet | Ask once more, in writing |
| Defensiveness or a new deadline | The refusal is the answer | Do not sign yet |

Then negotiate with [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/) and [whether to negotiate with a contractor](/guides/should-you-negotiate-with-a-contractor/), and move the agreed terms into a contract using the [contractor quote template](/guides/contractor-quote-template/). Keep [what to do when the final bill is higher than the quote](/guides/final-bill-higher-than-the-quote/) filed away as the document you hope never to need.

## The bottom line

Ten checks, in order, each resting on the layer above it. Skipping ahead to the total is how homeowners end up comparing numbers that were never comparable.
