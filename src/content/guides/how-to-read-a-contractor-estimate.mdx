**Read the exclusions first, the allowances second, the change-order terms third, and the total last.** That order is the whole method. The total is the least informative number on the page — two bids can say $28,000 and differ by $8,000 in what you actually get.

An estimate is a priced description of work: scope, materials, timeline, price. The Federal Trade Commission's baseline for a written estimate is exactly those four things — a floor, not a standard. Everything below that floor is where disputes and surprise costs are born.

## 1. Confirm the document is about your project

Start at the header: contractor name, address, license number, date, and estimate version. Then find the **scope of work**. It should describe the project you discussed — rooms, surfaces, systems, demolition, prep, installation, cleanup — not a category label like "kitchen renovation."

> If the scope statement doesn't match what you talked about, everything below it is negotiable by definition. Fix the scope before you read a single price.

Vague phrases (*standard materials*, *as discussed*, *repair as needed*, *per plan*) are prompts, not terms. Circle every one and ask for a written definition.

## 2. Break the numbers into three piles: labor, materials, allowances

A readable estimate separates **labor** from **materials**. Ask for the split on every line over about $2,000 — you cannot challenge a number you cannot decompose.

**Allowances** are placeholder budgets for items you haven't selected yet: tile, fixtures, cabinets, counters, lighting. An allowance is honest when it says the amount and what it assumes. It becomes a trap when it's low enough to make the bid look competitive and the real selection later arrives as a change order.

Watch for three patterns:

| Pattern | What it looks like | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Bundled line | "Flooring: $14,200" with no rate | Break it into material per sq ft + labor |
| Low allowance | "$8/sq ft tile allowance" | What does that price actually buy? |
| Rate inconsistency | Same work at two different unit rates | Which rate applies, and why? |

## 3. Read exclusions before you read the total

Exclusions define what is *not* in the price: permits, demolition, disposal, painting after the trades, moving appliances, repairs to whatever is found behind the wall.

- **A long exclusions list is not a bad sign** — it means the contractor anticipated site conditions. An estimate with no exclusions section has usually left those items to be discovered as change orders later.
- **A missing permit line on permitted work is a red flag.** Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether it's inside the total.
- **The cheapest bid often excludes the most.** That is why you normalize scope before comparing totals.

## 4. Payment schedule: milestones, not calendar dates

A defendable schedule ties payments to completed work: a deposit, progress payments at defined milestones, and a final payment after completion and your satisfaction. The FTC's guidance is blunt: don't pay the full amount up front, don't make the final payment until the work is done, and know that some states legally cap the down payment — contact your state or local consumer agency for your limit.

Front-loaded schedules — large deposits, or payments due before meaningful work exists — put your money at risk before the contractor has shown anything. One more reason the payment section matters more than the total.

## 5. Change orders: where budgets actually balloon

Find the change-order clause in the terms. The healthy version: changes must be **written, priced, and signed before work proceeds**, with a stated hourly rate and a stated markup on materials for anything added. The unhealthy version: "additional work billed at time-and-materials rates" with no rates defined — a blank check.

Ask for a cap on undefined time-and-materials rates. A contractor who won't put rates in writing is telling you their margin on change orders is higher than on the base quote.

## 6. Timeline, warranty, exclusions checklist

- **Timeline:** start assumptions, completion date, and what counts as "completion" (punch list done + final inspection passed is the standard definition).
- **Warranty:** labor warranty and manufacturer warranty listed separately, with durations. A 90-day labor warranty on a major renovation is weak; 1–5 years is common.
- **Terms:** cancellation right (the FTC's cooling-off rule gives you three business days to cancel when you sign at a location other than the contractor's permanent place of business), dispute resolution, and lien language. Blank spaces should be filled before signing.

## 7. Compare like for like — then decide

Put the estimates side by side against **one written scope**. Where one bid includes something another omits, ask the omitting contractor whether it's excluded, forgotten, or priced elsewhere — and get the answer in writing. That single step surfaces most of the real difference between competing bids.

Then, and only then, look at the totals. Run your number through the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) to see where it sits against published national ranges, and negotiate with the [questions every homeowner should ask before signing](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/).

## The bottom line

Read the estimate twice: once for what's included, once for what's missing. Exclusions, allowances, change orders, payment schedule — in that order — tell you what the number means. The total is just the last line of that story.
