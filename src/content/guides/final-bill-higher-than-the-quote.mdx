**When the final bill exceeds the signed quote, read the contract's change-order clause first, ask in writing for the signed authorization behind every extra charge, compare the invoice line by line against the original scope, and only then choose a dispute route — because the difference between a legitimate change order and an unauthorized overage is paperwork, not opinion.**

A higher invoice is a documentation problem before it is a money problem. The documents decide the outcome: what the scope said, what the change-order clause requires, and whether anyone signed anything before the extra work began.

## Start with the change-order clause

Open the contract and find the clause that governs changes. The protective version requires every change to be written, priced, and signed by both parties before work proceeds — the standard that Maine's Attorney General states for home construction contracts, and that Connecticut's home improvement statute requires for each change in the terms and conditions.

Then ask for records, in writing: every signed change order, the date each was approved, and the rate sheet or line-item price attached to it. An invoice that cannot produce those documents has nothing to rest on.

> The question is not whether the work was done. It is whether anyone agreed to the price before the work started.

## Match the invoice to the quote line by line

Take the original scope and the invoice side by side and classify every line. Most disputes resolve in this step, because the disputed amount is usually a handful of lines rather than the whole bill.

| Invoice line | Match against | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Amount equals a quoted line | Original quote | Billed as agreed |
| Amount exceeds a quoted line, signed change order attached | Signed change order | Approved change |
| Work listed in the exclusions, charged separately | Exclusions list | Allowed if excluded |
| Amount with no quote line and no change order | Original scope | Unauthorized overage |
| Rate never stated in the contract | Terms section | Disputed: no agreed rate |
| Allowance overrun | Allowance statement | Check the stated assumption |

The reading method for the original document is in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/), and the warning signs that predicted this conversation are catalogued in [red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/).

## Approved changes versus unauthorized overages

A legitimate increase is one you requested or one driven by a condition that could not be seen in advance, and it arrives as a priced document signed before the changed work began — the definition of a proper [change order](/guides/change-orders-why-the-price-goes-up/). An unauthorized overage is an amount that appears only on the final invoice, or a rate that was never written down anywhere.

Allowance overruns sit between the two. They are contractual if the selection genuinely exceeded a stated allowance and the assumption behind it was disclosed; they are not if the allowance was set low enough to make the original bid look competitive. That distinction is decided by the written assumption, not by the conversation.

## Dispute routes, in the order to use them

1. **Written demand.** Send a short letter that states the quoted price, the amount billed, the missing change orders, and the specific amount you are disputing. Keep a copy; dated correspondence is what later steps are built on.
2. **Your state or local consumer agency.** They can accept a complaint and, in some states, open mediation. This is a process step, not a legal conclusion.
3. **Credit card billing dispute.** The FTC explains that the Fair Credit Billing Act treats charges you did not accept, that were not delivered as agreed, that involved the wrong amount, or that were unauthorized as billing errors. Send a dispute letter to your card issuer at the billing-disputes address on your statement; the issuer must acknowledge within 30 days and resolve within two billing cycles, no more than 90 days, and you do not need to pay the disputed amount and related charges while it is under investigation — though you do need to pay the part of the bill that is not in question. Quality complaints are not billing errors, and debit card payments have different protections, so contact your issuer without delay.
4. **Small claims court.** Limits and procedures vary by state; check your state's courts website before assuming anything about eligibility or timelines.
5. **A licensed attorney in your state.** For a large amount or a stalled file, this is the correct next call rather than the first one.

## Before you pay the balance

Pay the undisputed portion if the contract supports it, hold the disputed portion until you have the authorization records or a written resolution, and put every agreement in writing before releasing anything. Confirm the scope you originally signed for with the sequence in [how to review a contractor quote](/guides/how-to-review-a-contractor-quote/), and use the [contractor quote template](/guides/contractor-quote-template/) on your next project so the change-order section exists before work starts. The questions that surface this clause early are in [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/).

## The bottom line

Match every extra charge to a signed document; pay what the paperwork supports and dispute what it does not, in writing, in the order above. A final bill that cannot show its change orders is a bill waiting to be answered, not paid.
