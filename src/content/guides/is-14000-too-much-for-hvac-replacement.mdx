**For a full furnace-plus-AC replacement, $14,000 sits inside the middle of published national ranges — it is not automatically high; what decides is system size, efficiency tier, ductwork condition, and whether the quote itemizes those.**

A single number cannot tell you whether a price is fair, because the same number buys different scopes. What you can do is place the quote against published national ranges, then check whether the quote shows the pieces that move those ranges.

## 1. What published national cost guides say (as of October 2026)

Published guides do not agree with each other — they use different assumptions about house size, ductwork, and equipment tier — so read them as bands, not verdicts:

| Published guide | Reported band | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| HomeGuide | $5,000–$11,000; $7,000–$16,000 once ductwork is involved | HVAC replacement including furnace and AC |
| Fixr | $7,500–$21,000 with existing ducts in good condition; $9,400–$27,000 with new ducts | Replacing furnace and central AC together |
| Angi | $5,000–$12,500, reaching about $22,000 with new ductwork and added features | HVAC system replacement |

> Read the table this way: $14,000 falls above the plain equipment-replacement bands and inside the bands that include ductwork. The question the quote has to answer is which of those two situations you are in.

As of October 2026, published national cost guides report full-system replacement commonly landing roughly $5,000–$15,000+, with ductwork, size, and efficiency moving the number. These are planning ranges for a national audience, not a price for your house — your local labor market, home size, and existing setup set the actual figure.

## 2. What makes a quote legitimately higher

Some of the gap between quotes is real scope. Look for these lines, and check that each one is named:

- **Ductwork.** Replacement or modification of ducts is a large, common driver, and it is the reason published bands widen at the top.
- **Electrical work.** A new circuit or a panel capacity upgrade is a legitimate price line when the project calls for it.
- **Efficiency tier.** A premium variable-speed system costs more than an entry-level model of the same size. Ask for model numbers and ratings so you can compare tiers across quotes.
- **Equipment access.** Where the equipment sits and how it reaches its location affects labor hours.
- **Permits and disposal.** Permit fees and haul-away of the old equipment are separate costs that a low bid may simply exclude.

None of these are opinions about your system. They are scope items that explain a price, and each one should be visible on the page rather than implied.

## 3. Which line items to challenge

Challenge what you cannot decompose:

1. **The single lump sum.** "HVAC replacement: $14,000" should become equipment, labor, ductwork, electrical, permits, and disposal.
2. **Unpriced ductwork allowances.** "Repair as needed" with no amount is a placeholder that arrives later as a change order.
3. **Missing permit line.** Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether the fee is inside the total.
4. **Unlabeled contingency.** A "miscellaneous" or "reserve" line without a description and a stated rate is an undefined price.
5. **Markup you can't see.** Materials marked up without a stated percentage are covered in [what is a fair markup on materials](/guides/what-is-a-fair-markup-on-materials/), including how to spot the same material being charged twice.
6. **A front-loaded payment schedule.** Large deposits or payments due before meaningful work exists put money at risk early. The questions to ask before signing cover how to read that section.

The rest of the method — scope first, exclusions next, allowances, then the total — is in how to read a contractor estimate.

## 4. Get three quotes, then compare scope before totals

One quote gives you a number; three give you a range for your house. The standard rule of thumb is three written quotes built against the same scope, so the comparison measures price rather than differences in what is included — see [how many contractor quotes should I get](/guides/how-many-contractor-quotes-should-i-get/) and [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/).

When the three totals are in front of you, run each one through the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) to see where it sits against published ranges.

Questions about whether a system or an installation is safe, correctly sized, or compliant with local code are outside what a price guide can answer — consult your local building authority or a licensed professional.

## The bottom line

$14,000 is inside published national bands for a full-system replacement as of October 2026, so the number alone does not settle the question. What settles it is whether the quote breaks out equipment, ductwork, electrical, permits, and disposal — and whether two more quotes written against the same scope land in the same neighborhood.
