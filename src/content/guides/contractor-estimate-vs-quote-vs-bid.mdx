**An estimate is a non-binding projection, a quote or bid is a fixed offer for a defined scope, and once you sign it, it typically becomes the contract that governs the job.**

The three words get used interchangeably in conversation, and that looseness is expensive. Each document carries a different level of commitment, a different level of detail, and a different amount of protection for the number you are about to pay. Knowing which one is on the table tells you how much of the price is real.

## The three documents side by side

| | Estimate | Quote / Bid | Contract |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A projection of likely cost | A fixed offer for a stated scope | An agreement with terms both sides accept |
| Binding? | Usually not; priced before scope is fixed | Firm for its stated validity period, subject to its own terms | Binding according to its wording and your state's rules |
| Price precision | Range or best guess | Number you can plan on | Number plus rules for how it can change |
| Typical detail | Categories and assumptions | Itemized lines, allowances, exclusions, schedule | Scope, payment schedule, change orders, warranty, cancellation |
| Best used for | Early budgeting | Choosing a contractor | Starting work |

Language varies by trade and region — some firms call the same document an estimate, others a proposal. The words matter less than the substance: is the price a guess or an offer, and does the document say what happens if the job changes?

## What each document leaves open

**The estimate leaves the scope open.** It is honest at the stage it is issued: nobody has selected the tile, opened the wall, or confirmed the prep work. Its number is a direction, not a destination. Trouble starts when an estimate is treated as a price after the scope has been decided.

**The quote leaves nothing open — on purpose.** It names inclusions, allowances, and exclusions, states a validity date, and holds that number for the period it says. That is the document you can build a budget around.

**The contract leaves the rules open if you let it.** A signed agreement with blank spaces, undefined rates, or no change-order clause hands every later decision to whoever drafts it next. The FTC's advice is to read the contract before signing it, make sure every blank space is filled, and confirm it includes the contractor's details, start and completion dates, and any promises made during your conversations.

## Ask for an itemized fixed-price quote in writing

The phrase worth using with any contractor is simple: **"Please send an itemized fixed-price quote in writing."** Each word does a job:

- **Itemized** — line-by-line breakdowns you can challenge individually, not one bundled total per room.
- **Fixed price** — the number holds for the stated scope, rather than drifting with labor or material movement during the job.
- **Quote** — an offer, not a projection, with a validity date attached.
- **In writing** — the FTC's baseline for an estimate is that it describes the work, materials, completion date, and price; a quote should meet that floor and then add exclusions, allowances, and payment terms.

Two supporting requests finish the set: who pulls the permit and whether the fee is inside the total, and what the change-order rates are for anything added later. The full reading order for the document is in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/).

## The price-protection angle

The gap between an estimate and a fixed quote is where budgets break.

- **On an estimate**, the price can be revised as details settle — legitimately, because information changed, and occasionally because the original number was built to be attractive rather than accurate.
- **On a fixed quote**, the price does not move unless something in the scope moves, and then it moves through a written change order with defined rates.

That is the protection: not a promise that nothing will ever change, but an agreement that changes arrive as documents you approve, not as invoices you discover. An open-ended clause — "additional work billed at time-and-materials rates" with no rates stated — removes the protection entirely, which is one of the [red flags worth catching before signature](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/).

Watch also for a quote with no validity date. A price that never expires sounds generous; a price that expires in days exists to stop you from comparing. Either way, ask what happens if the start date moves — a fixed quote with a clear re-pricing rule for long delays is more honest than a silent one.

## From quote to contract

If the contractor hands you a quote and asks for a signature, treat that moment as the contract moment. Before it, confirm that scope, payment schedule, warranty, timeline, and cancellation rights are all in the document, along with any verbal promises you were made — the FTC specifically says promises about scope and cost made during conversations should end up in the agreement. Our [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/) collects them in one list, and [the quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) gives you a published-range check on the number itself.

State rules differ on what a signed document must contain and when you may cancel. When a question turns on local requirements, consult your local building authority or a licensed professional, or check general consumer guidance such as [Nolo](https://www.nolo.com).

## The bottom line

An estimate tells you a direction, a quote tells you a number, and your signature tells you which rules apply. Ask for the itemized fixed-price quote in writing, keep the change-order rates inside it, and read it as the contract it may already be.
