**Verify on the state contractor licensing board's own website — not on the contractor's site — matching the exact business name, license number, status, and bond and insurance, because every state runs its own board with its own rules.**

Licensing in the United States is not a single system. It is fifty separate systems plus local ones, each with its own records, classifications, and status labels. Verification is a records check you perform yourself, and it takes about five minutes when you have the license number.

## 1. Why the board is the only primary source

The board's public lookup is the record. Everything else — a badge on a website, a photo of a certificate, a line in a proposal — is a copy, and copies do not show their date.

Two practical rules follow:

1. **Get the license number in writing before the lookup,** in the proposal or the contract, not from a business card.
2. **Open the board's site yourself and type the number in.** If the contractor wants to show you the record on their screen, that is fine as a conversation starter; it is not your verification.

The FTC's home improvement guidance puts this alongside the written estimate and the signed contract: checks you perform before money moves, not after.

## 2. What to match, field by field

| Field on the quote | What to match on the board site |
|---|---|
| Business name | The exact legal entity, plus any assumed or "t/a" name the crew uses |
| License number | The same number belongs to this entity, not a parent company or another person |
| Classification | The license class covers the type of work you are hiring |
| Status | Active — not expired, suspended, revoked, or inactive |
| Expiration | Valid through your planned start and completion dates |
| Bond | Current bond listed, with an amount and expiration date |
| Insurance | Checked separately — many boards do not verify insurance for you |

**Name variants are the most common mismatch.** "Smith Renovation LLC," "Smith Renovation," and "J. Smith Home Improvement" may be three different records, or one record with a DBA. If the entity signing the contract is not the entity on the license, ask why in writing before proceeding.

**Classification is a scope question.** Boards publish what each classification is authorized to cover, and many states require a license for some trades and only a registration or a local permit for others. Read the board's own classification description for your project type rather than relying on a category label the contractor chose.

**Insurance is not the license.** Worker's compensation and general liability are separate checks: ask for certificates and confirm them with the insurer or agent named on the certificate. Treat that as a document check, not a judgment about the work.

## 3. Example boards, and where each state differs

| State | Who licenses contractors | Where to verify |
|---|---|---|
| California | Contractors State License Board (CSLB) | [cslb.ca.gov](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/) |
| Texas | Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) | [tdlr.texas.gov](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/) |
| Florida | Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) | [myfloridalicense.com](https://www.myfloridalicense.com/) |
| New York | No single statewide contractor license — licensing is local | Check your city, county, or town consumer affairs office |

New York is the honest exception worth spelling out: there is no single statewide contractor license. Home improvement contractor licensing there runs through localities — New York City, Nassau, Suffolk, Westchester, Putnam, Rockland counties, and the City of Buffalo are the commonly cited examples — so in New York you verify with the locality where the work happens. Other states fall somewhere between these models: some license general contractors statewide, some license only certain trades, and some leave broad categories to local registration.

If you cannot find your state on a list like this, search your state's official site for the agency that publishes contractor licenses, and confirm you are on a `.gov` domain before entering anything.

## 4. When the license is expired, suspended, or pulled by another entity

Status labels are published so you can read them:

- **Expired.** Some boards allow a renewal window. Check the effective dates on the record and ask for confirmation of renewal once it posts — not a screenshot from before.
- **Suspended or revoked.** The board acted. Read the disciplinary record the board publishes alongside the status, and ask the contractor to explain it in writing.
- **Held by a different person or entity.** Common when a company operates under someone else's license. Confirm that the entity signing your contract is the entity on the license, and ask how the working relationship is documented.
- **No record at all.** If the board has no match for the number you were given, you have your answer about whether that license exists. For what to do next, contact the board directly or your local consumer protection office.

## 5. What a license does and does not tell you

> A license tells you the state has a record of this business and what discipline, if any, appears on it. It does not tell you the price is fair, the work will be good, or the installed system is correct.

A license is a consumer-rights check, not an endorsement. It confirms registration and gives you a public file to read — that is the entire claim.
