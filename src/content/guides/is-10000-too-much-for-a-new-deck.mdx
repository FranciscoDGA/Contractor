**For a new deck, $10,000 sits above the published project averages from Angi and HomeAdvisor, inside their $4,340 to $12,652 range, inside Fixr's $9,000 to $20,000 band, and inside HomeGuide's $6,000 to $12,000 band for a 12-by-20 deck as of October 2026 — so the number is ordinary for a mid-size deck, and the quote has to show square footage and material.**

A single number cannot tell you whether a price is fair, because the same number buys different scopes. Place the quote against published national ranges, then check whether it shows the pieces that move those ranges.

## 1. What published national cost guides say (as of October 2026)

Published guides disagree because they assume different sizes, materials, and features, so read them as bands, not verdicts:

| Published guide | Reported band | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| HomeGuide | $25–$50 per square foot installed; 12-by-20 deck $6,000–$12,000 | New deck, materials and labor |
| Fixr | $9,000–$20,000, average $14,000; $15 to $55 per square foot | Deck size, materials, features, local labor |
| Angi | $4,340–$12,652, average $8,319 | 20,403 verified projects, 2026 at $30 to $60 per square foot |
| HomeAdvisor | $4,340–$12,653, average $8,318 | Deck size, materials, and design complexity |

> Read the table this way. $10,000 sits above both project averages, inside both project ranges, at the bottom of HomeGuide's 20-by-20 band, and in the upper half of its 12-by-20 band — so it is ordinary, and the quote has to show square footage and material.

Angi and HomeAdvisor publish near-identical figures, so treat those two rows as one measurement. These are planning ranges for a national audience, not a price for your deck — local labor, footprint, and material set the figure.

## 2. What makes a quote legitimately higher

Some of the gap between quotes is real scope. Check that each of these lines is named:

- **Square footage.** HomeGuide prices a 12-by-12 at $3,600 to $7,200 and a 20-by-20 at $10,000 to $20,000; Angi puts a 16-by-20 at $12,800 to $19,200. Footprint alone explains most of a gap.
- **Material.** HomeGuide's installed prices run $25 to $50 for pressure-treated pine, $30 to $47 for cedar, and $40 to $80 for composite. Ask for the exact product line — "composite" without a name is not a specification.
- **Height, stairs, and railing.** Angi says ground-level decks land near the lower end of its $30 to $60 per square foot while second-story builds push higher. Stairs and railing are priced separately from the decking surface and are common exclusions in a low total.
- **Permits.** Angi lists permit fees at $230 to $500, and HomeAdvisor puts building permits at $350 to $1,800. Ask who pulls the permit, who pays, and whether it is inside the total.
- **Removal of an old deck.** HomeGuide prices deck replacement at $30 to $55 per square foot, above the $25 to $50 it publishes for new construction.

None of these are opinions about your deck — they are scope items that should be visible on the page rather than implied.

## 3. Which line items to challenge

Challenge what you cannot decompose:

1. **The single lump sum.** "New deck: $10,000" should become square footage, material, framing, stairs, railing, permits, and removal.
2. **An unpriced material or railing allowance.** "Railing as selected" with no amount is a placeholder that arrives later as a change order — see [change orders and why the price goes up](/guides/change-orders-why-the-price-goes-up/).
3. **A missing permit line.** Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether the fee is inside the total.
4. **An unlabeled contingency.** A "miscellaneous" line without a description and a stated rate is an undefined price.
5. **Markup you cannot see.** Materials marked up without a stated percentage are covered in [what is a fair markup on materials](/guides/what-is-a-fair-markup-on-materials/).
6. **A front-loaded payment schedule.** Payments due before meaningful work exists put money at risk early; down-payment limits vary by state.

The rest of the method — scope first, exclusions next, allowances, then the total — is in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/).

## 4. Get three quotes, then compare scope before totals

One quote gives you a number; three give you a range for your yard. The rule of thumb is three written quotes built against the same scope, so the comparison measures price rather than differences in what is included — see [how many contractor quotes should I get](/guides/how-many-contractor-quotes-should-i-get/) and [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/).

Run each total through the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) to see where it sits against published ranges. The same comparison works for roofing — [is $15,000 too much for roof replacement](/guides/is-15000-too-much-for-roof-replacement/) shows the method.

Questions about permits, how a deck should be built, or local code sit outside a price guide — consult your local building authority or a licensed professional.

## 5. Payment and contract terms worth reading before you sign

The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on home improvement scams says to get estimates in writing, read the contract before signing it, fill in every blank space, and never make the final payment until the work is done. [Red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/) maps that advice onto the document, and [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/) has the wording worth requesting in writing. A quote with a deadline attached asks you to skip competing estimates and a contract read at your own pace — do not sign yet.

## The bottom line

$10,000 is inside published national bands for a new deck as of October 2026, so the number alone does not settle it. What settles it is whether the quote shows square footage, material by product name, stairs, railing, permits, and removal — and whether two more quotes on the same scope land in the same neighborhood.
