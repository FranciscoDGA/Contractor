**The clearest red flags are no written scope, front-loaded payments, a missing permit line on permitted work, a refusal to itemize, open-ended change-order terms, and pressure to sign today.**

A quote is a document about money and process, and the warning signs live in its structure. Six of them show up on the page itself; a second set comes from how the contractor behaves around the document. Both sets are documented in the Federal Trade Commission's consumer guidance on home improvement.

## The six on-page red flags

| Red flag | What it looks like | What it signals |
|---|---|---|
| No written scope | "Kitchen remodel" with a single number | The job description is undefined, so the price is too |
| Front-loaded payments | Large deposit, or payments due before meaningful work | Your money is at risk before anything exists on site |
| Missing permit line | No mention of permits, fees, or inspections | Who handles it and who pays is unresolved |
| Refusal to itemize | Bundled totals on every line | No number can be checked, so no number can be challenged |
| Open-ended change orders | "Additional work at time-and-materials rates" with no rates | The margin on extras is undefined by design |
| Pressure to sign today | Discount expires tonight, crew is available only now | The deadline substitutes for the due diligence |

Each of these is a process problem, not a judgment about anyone's skill. The fix is the same every time: ask for the missing term in writing before anything is signed.

## The FTC's list: behavior around the quote

The FTC's article on home improvement scams names the tactics scammers use to get money and a signature:

- **Door-to-door pressure.** Contractors who knock because they are "in the area," or who say they have materials left over from a previous job.
- **Pressure for an immediate decision.** Any urgency attached to a quote exists to shorten your thinking time.
- **Cash or full payment up front.** Requests to pay for everything before work starts, or to deal only in cash.
- **Asking you to get the permits.** The FTC lists this as a scam tactic — the party doing the work is normally the party engaging with the permit process.
- **Financing through a lender they know.** The FTC describes a loan scam in which the homeowner signs papers for a home equity loan with unfavorable terms, sometimes before the work is finished.

The FTC's protective advice runs alongside the list: consider only contractors who are licensed and insured, verify the license with your state or county, get estimates in writing, read the contract before signing it, and make sure every blank space is filled in.

> A quote that arrives with a deadline attached is asking you to skip the steps that protect you: competing estimates, a written scope, and a contract read at your own pace.

## Money patterns worth stopping on

Payment terms are where quotes cause the most damage, so they deserve their own pass.

- **A deposit sized by anxiety, not by work.** Progress payments tied to milestones keep money and progress aligned; payments tied to dates do not.
- **A final payment that comes early.** The FTC's guidance is blunt: never make the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied with it.
- **A down payment above your state's limit.** Some states legally cap the amount a contractor can request as a down payment. Contact your state or local consumer agency to learn the limit where you live.

None of these terms is a verdict on the contractor. They are terms, and terms can be rewritten before signature — see [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/) for where each one sits in the document.

## Paperwork and permit questions

Two documentation gaps cause avoidable disputes:

**No license or insurance details on the quote.** Ask for the license number and proof of insurance, then verify the license with your state or county government rather than accepting a card or a photo.

**A silent permit section.** Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether the fee is included in the total. If you are unsure whether your project needs a permit or an inspection, consult your local building authority or a licensed professional — that determination is not ours to make.

## When a red flag is just a missing sentence

Not every gap is a character flaw. Contractors who write quotes on a pad between jobs leave things out, and a long exclusions list is often a sign of experience rather than evasion. The test is how the contractor responds to a written request:

- **Responds with the missing term** — keep going.
- **Responds with an explanation but no writing** — ask once more in writing.
- **Responds with defensiveness or a deadline** — treat that as the information you needed.

## The bottom line

Read a quote for what it refuses to put on paper. Missing scope, front-loaded money, undefined change orders, and a ticking clock are all decisions someone made before the document reached you — and each one is cheaper to fix before signature than after work starts.
