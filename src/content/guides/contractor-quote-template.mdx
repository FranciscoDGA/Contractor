**A complete written quote carries fifteen fields — parties, property, scope, line items, materials with allowances, labor, exclusions, permit responsibility, payment schedule, change-order terms, timeline, warranty, cleanup and disposal, license and insurance, and a validity period with a signature block — and every one of them exists to prevent a specific argument later.**

A quote is a price attached to a description. When the description is thin, the price is unenforceable in practice: there is nothing to measure a later invoice against. The checklist below is the structure to look for, or to ask for, on any job worth writing down: copy the field list, send it ahead of the site visit, and ask the contractor to fill it in.

## The fifteen fields, in order

1. **Parties.** Contractor legal name, address, and license number, plus your name.
2. **Job location.** The address the work happens at, which is not always the billing address.
3. **Scope of work.** Rooms, surfaces, systems, demo, prep, installation, and cleanup in plain description.
4. **Line items.** Each major line priced separately, with labor split from materials.
5. **Materials and allowances.** Grade named where possible, plus an allowance stated as an amount and its assumption.
6. **Labor.** How labor is charged: fixed per line, or hourly with a stated rate.
7. **Exclusions.** A written list of what is not in the price.
8. **Permit responsibility.** Who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether the fee is inside the total.
9. **Payment schedule.** Deposit, milestone-based progress payments, final payment after completion.
10. **Change-order terms.** Written, priced, and signed before work proceeds, with stated rates.
11. **Timeline.** Start date, completion date, and what counts as completion.
12. **Warranty.** Labor warranty and manufacturer warranty listed separately, each with a duration.
13. **Cleanup and disposal.** Who hauls debris, who pays the dump fee, when the site is broom-clean.
14. **License and insurance.** License number plus proof of liability coverage, not a verbal assurance.
15. **Validity period and signature.** How long the price holds, the date, and both signature lines.

## What each field protects you from

| Field | What it protects you from | What its absence signals |
|---|---|---|
| Parties and license number | Paying an unverifiable business | The business cannot be checked |
| Job location | Which property is covered | Ambiguity is deliberate or careless |
| Scope of work | Charging for work you thought was included | The description is left open by design |
| Line items | A single number nobody can challenge | Refusal to itemize is the pattern |
| Materials and allowances | A low placeholder becoming a surcharge | The bid was priced to look competitive |
| Labor | Rates invented after the work is done | The margin sits in the undefined part |
| Exclusions | Surprise charges for omitted work | Everything excluded is a future invoice |
| Permit responsibility | Fees and delays landing on you | The topic was avoided on purpose |
| Payment schedule | Money at risk before work exists | Front-loading is the point |
| Change-order terms | Open-ended time-and-materials billing | The margin on extras is undefined |
| Timeline | Delays with no reference point | No date means no accountability |
| Warranty | Promises that expire when you need them | A warranty without a duration is talk |
| Cleanup and disposal | Dump fees appearing at the end | The least visible line is easiest to add |
| License and insurance | Hiring someone who cannot be pursued | The check was never offered |
| Validity and signature | Repricing after you have compared bids | The number was never final |

## Fields that state law may already require

Some of these are not courtesies. California's Contractors State License Board states that a home improvement contract must contain specific terms, and that a down payment may not exceed $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. Maine's Attorney General lists the statutory parts of a home construction contract over $3,000, including a written change-order requirement. Pennsylvania's Attorney General requires a written, signed contract for home improvements over $500.

> The fields are not paperwork for their own sake. Each one replaces a conversation that would otherwise be remembered differently six weeks later.

Whether a project needs a permit is a question for your local building authority or a licensed professional. ## Quote, estimate, or contract

This template describes a price document, not the agreement that governs the job. An [estimate, a quote, and a bid](/guides/contractor-estimate-vs-quote-vs-bid/) carry different promises, and many contractors treat a signed quote as binding — so ask for a contract before signing anything, and move scope, payment schedule, change-order terms, and warranty into it. The fifteen fields tell you what that contract has to contain.

## How to use the template

Send the fifteen fields as a list before the walkthrough, ask for the finished document in writing, and hold each answer to the place it belongs: scope and exclusions in the body, allowances in a list of their own, milestones with amounts, change orders in the terms, warranty in its own clause. The review order that surrounds this checklist is in [how to review a contractor quote](/guides/how-to-review-a-contractor-quote/), the questions worth asking before signature are in [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/), and the license is yours to check with [how to verify a contractor license](/guides/how-to-verify-a-contractor-license/).

## The bottom line

A quote is complete when all fifteen fields are filled in, dated, and signed by both parties, and incomplete when any of them is blank, verbal, or deferred to a later conversation. Missing fields are cheaper to fix before signature than after the first load of materials arrives.
