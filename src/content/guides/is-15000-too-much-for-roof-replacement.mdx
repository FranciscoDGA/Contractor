**For a full roof replacement, $15,000 sits inside HomeGuide's and Angi's published bands as of October 2026, above Fixr's entire $7,500 to $14,000 band, and just under This Old House's $15,439 survey figure for a 2,000-square-foot asphalt shingle roof — so the number alone does not settle the question, and the quote's line items do.**

A single number cannot tell you whether a price is fair, because the same number buys different scopes. Place the quote against published national ranges, then check whether it shows the pieces that move those ranges.

## 1. What published national cost guides say (as of October 2026)

Published guides disagree because they assume different roof sizes, materials, and tear-off, so read them as bands, not verdicts:

| Published guide | Reported band | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| HomeGuide | $5,700–$16,000, national average $10,800 | Asphalt roof replacement, materials and installation |
| Fixr | $7,500–$14,000, average about $10,000 | Roof replacement by size, material, roof design, local labor |
| Angi | $5,902–$46,000, average $9,609 | Professional roof replacement across all materials and roof sizes |
| This Old House | $15,439 average | 2026 survey of 1,000 homeowners who replaced a roof |

> Read the table this way. $15,000 sits above three of the four averages, just under This Old House's figure for a 2,000-square-foot asphalt roof, and above Fixr's entire published band — so the quote has to answer which situation you actually have.

HomeGuide prices the work at $300 to $600 per roofing square of 100 square feet including materials and installation, with roofing labor at $150 to $300 per square, and Angi publishes the same job at $4 to $11 per square foot. These are planning ranges for a national audience, not a price for your house — local labor, roof size, and material set the actual figure.

## 2. What makes a quote legitimately higher

Some of the gap between quotes is real scope. Check that each of these lines is named:

- **Roof area.** Roofers price by the square, and roof surface exceeds floor area, so a bigger footprint and a complex roofline add labor hours.
- **Material tier.** Angi publishes asphalt shingle replacement at $5,800 to $20,000 installed and high-end slate at $5,800 to $30,000. Ask for the brand and product line so tiers compare across quotes.
- **Tear-off and disposal.** Removing the existing roof and hauling it away is a line a low bid may exclude.
- **Decking replacement.** An allowance here should carry a stated price per square foot, not a promise to look once the old roof is off.
- **Permits.** Angi's shingle roof guide lists permit fees at $250 to $500. Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether it is inside the total.

None of these are opinions about your roof — they are scope items, and each should be visible on the page rather than implied.

## 3. Which line items to challenge

Challenge what you cannot decompose:

1. **The single lump sum.** "Roof replacement: $15,000" should become tear-off, materials, labor, decking allowance, permits, and disposal.
2. **An unpriced decking allowance.** "Repair as needed" with no amount is a placeholder that arrives later as a change order — see [change orders and why the price goes up](/guides/change-orders-why-the-price-goes-up/).
3. **A missing permit line.** Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether the fee is inside the total.
4. **An unlabeled contingency.** A "miscellaneous" line without a description and a stated rate is an undefined price.
5. **Markup you cannot see.** Materials marked up without a stated percentage are covered in [what is a fair markup on materials](/guides/what-is-a-fair-markup-on-materials/).
6. **A front-loaded payment schedule.** Payments due before meaningful work exists put money at risk early, and down-payment limits vary by state.

The rest of the method — scope first, exclusions next, allowances, then the total — is in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/).

## 4. Get three quotes, then compare scope before totals

One quote gives you a number; three give you a range for your house. The standard rule of thumb is three written quotes built against the same scope, so the comparison measures price rather than differences in what is included — see [how many contractor quotes should I get](/guides/how-many-contractor-quotes-should-i-get/) and [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/).

Run each of the three totals through the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) to see where it sits against published ranges. The same band comparison works for windows — [is $12,000 too much for window replacement](/guides/is-12000-too-much-for-window-replacement/) walks through it there.

Questions about whether a roof needs work, how it should be installed, or what local code requires sit outside a price guide — consult your local building authority or a licensed professional.

## 5. Payment and contract terms worth reading before you sign

The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on home improvement scams says to get estimates in writing, read the contract before signing it, fill in every blank space, and never make the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied. [Red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/) maps that advice onto the document itself. A quote with a deadline attached is asking you to skip competing estimates and a contract read at your own pace — do not sign yet, and use [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/) for the wording worth requesting in writing.

## The bottom line

$15,000 is above Fixr's published band but inside HomeGuide's and Angi's as of October 2026, so the number alone does not settle the question. What settles it is whether the quote breaks out tear-off, materials, decking, permits, and disposal — and whether two more quotes written against the same scope land in the same neighborhood.
