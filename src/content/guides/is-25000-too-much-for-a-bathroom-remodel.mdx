**For a full bathroom remodel, $25,000 sits at the ceiling of HomeGuide's published full-remodel band and at the floor of Angi's full-scope band as of October 2026, well above Fixr's $6,000 to $18,000 typical range — so it belongs only if the quote is buying a full remodel rather than a partial update.**

A single number cannot tell you whether a price is fair, because the same number buys different scopes. Place the quote against published national ranges, then check whether it shows the pieces that move those ranges.

## 1. What published national cost guides say (as of October 2026)

Published guides disagree because they measure different room sizes, scopes, and finish levels, so read them as bands, not verdicts:

| Published guide | Reported band | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| HomeGuide | $8,800–$25,000+ full, $5,300–$15,000 partial, $3,500–$7,500 minor | 35- to 50-square-foot bathroom at $100 to $500 per square foot |
| Fixr | $6,000–$18,000, average $12,000 | Bathroom size, material quality, fixtures |
| Angi | $2,500–$30,000, average $12,151; full scope $25,000–$80,000 | 2026 cost data at $70 to $250 per square foot |
| This Old House | $6,456–$24,715, average $15,586 | 2026 survey of 1,000 homeowners, rooms of 40 to 150 square feet |

> Read the table this way. $25,000 sits at the ceiling of HomeGuide's full-remodel band, above Fixr's entire typical range and Angi's average, and at the floor of Angi's full-scope band — so the quote has to be a full remodel to belong there.

HomeGuide's own split is the useful test: minor work at $100 to $150 per square foot, partial at $150 to $300, full at $250 to $500. These are planning ranges for a national audience, not a price for your bathroom — local labor, room size, and finish level set the actual figure.

## 2. What makes a quote legitimately higher

Some of the gap between quotes is real scope. Check that each of these lines is named:

- **Room size.** HomeGuide puts a 5-by-10 full remodel at $12,500 to $25,000 and an 8-by-10 at $20,000 to $40,000, so square footage alone can explain most of a gap.
- **Tile scope.** Wall and floor tile are priced by the square foot and move a total more than fixture choice does. Ask for the square footage and the tile price.
- **Demolition.** Angi lists demolition at $1,000 to $2,300, separate from the rebuild.
- **Plumbing and electrical relocation.** This separates a partial from a full remodel in every published band, and it should carry its own price.
- **Permits.** Angi lists permit fees at $100 to $1,000. Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether it is inside the total.

None of these are opinions about your bathroom — they are scope items that should be visible on the page rather than implied.

## 3. Which line items to challenge

Challenge what you cannot decompose:

1. **The single lump sum.** "Bathroom remodel: $25,000" should become demolition, tile, fixtures, vanity, plumbing, electrical, labor, permits, and disposal.
2. **An unpriced tile or fixture allowance.** "Allowance as selected" with no amount is a placeholder that arrives later as a change order — see [change orders and why the price goes up](/guides/change-orders-why-the-price-goes-up/).
3. **A missing permit line.** Ask who pulls the permit, who pays the fee, and whether the fee is inside the total.
4. **An unlabeled contingency.** A "miscellaneous" line without a description and a stated rate is an undefined price.
5. **Markup you cannot see.** Materials marked up without a stated percentage are covered in [what is a fair markup on materials](/guides/what-is-a-fair-markup-on-materials/).
6. **A front-loaded payment schedule.** Payments due before meaningful work exists put money at risk early, and down-payment limits vary by state.

The rest of the method — scope first, exclusions next, allowances, then the total — is in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/).

## 4. Get three quotes, then compare scope before totals

One quote gives you a number; three give you a range for your bathroom. The standard rule of thumb is three written quotes built against the same scope, so the comparison measures price rather than differences in what is included — see [how many contractor quotes should I get](/guides/how-many-contractor-quotes-should-i-get/) and [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/).

Run each of the three totals through the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) to see where it sits against published ranges. This Old House's 2026 survey found about one in three homeowners said the project cost more than expected, which is what allowances in writing are meant to prevent. The same comparison works for kitchens — [is $45,000 too much for a kitchen remodel](/guides/is-45000-too-much-for-a-kitchen-remodel/) applies it there.

Questions about permits, how plumbing work should be done, or local code sit outside a price guide — consult your local building authority or a licensed professional.

## 5. Payment and contract terms worth reading before you sign

The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on home improvement scams says to get estimates in writing, read the contract before signing it, fill in every blank space, and never make the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied. [Red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/) maps that advice onto the document itself. A quote with a deadline attached asks you to skip competing estimates and a contract read at your own pace — do not sign yet.

## The bottom line

$25,000 is a full-remodel number as of October 2026, not a partial one — it sits inside the published full-scope bands while sitting above the typical project ranges. What settles the question is whether the quote breaks out demolition, tile, fixtures, plumbing, permits, and disposal, and whether two more quotes on the same scope land in the same neighborhood.
