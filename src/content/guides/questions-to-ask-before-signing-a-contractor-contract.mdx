**The questions that matter are about scope, payment milestones, permits, change orders, warranty, and your right to cancel — and every answer has to land in writing inside the contract, not in the conversation that preceded it.**

A contract conversation is where a project is defined. The written document is where it is enforced. Below are the questions to bring to the meeting, the answers that should concern you, and where each answer has to end up on the page.

## 1. The ten questions to bring with you

Bring this list on paper and ask them in order.

1. **"Can you put the full scope of work in this contract — rooms, surfaces, systems, demo, prep, cleanup — rather than a category label like 'kitchen renovation'?"**
   Scope is what everything else is measured against. Vague scope makes every later price arguable.

2. **"What is excluded from this price?"**
   Get the exclusions as a written list: permits, disposal, painting after the trades, appliance moves, repairs found behind walls.

3. **"Which items are allowances, and what does each allowance actually buy?"**
   An allowance is a placeholder budget. Ask for the amount and the assumption behind it, for tile, fixtures, cabinets, counters, and lighting.

4. **"What is the payment schedule, and what completed milestone does each payment depend on?"**
   You want a deposit, progress payments tied to defined milestones, and a final payment after completion.

5. **"How much is due at signing?"**
   The FTC advises against paying the full amount up front. Some states and localities cap down payments — check your state or local consumer agency for your own limit.

6. **"Who pulls the permits, who pays the fee, and is that fee inside this total?"**
   Get the answer in the exclusions or the price breakdown, whichever applies. Missing permit lines on permitted work are a common source of later invoices.

7. **"What is your change-order process — written, priced, and signed before work starts — and what hourly rate and material markup apply?"**
   You are asking for rates to be stated in advance. Undefined time-and-materials language with no rates is an open-ended price.

8. **"What is the start date, the completion date, and what counts as completion?"**
   Ask what defines done: punch list finished and final inspection passed is the standard definition.

9. **"What warranty do you give on labor, in writing, how long, and what does the manufacturer cover separately?"**
   Labor warranty and manufacturer warranty should appear as two distinct terms with durations.

10. **"Which trades are yours and which are subcontracted, and are the subcontractors' costs inside this price?"**
    Also worth asking at this point: whether the business carries worker's comp and liability insurance and can provide certificates. Verify the license yourself at the [state licensing board](/guides/how-to-verify-a-contractor-license/).

## 2. Answers that should worry you

Not every bad answer is dramatic. These are the ones that predict a larger final bill or a fight later:

| Answer you hear | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| "Just sign the estimate, that's basically the contract" | The scope, change-order rules, and payment terms have not been written down |
| "We'll figure out the extras as we go" | Change orders arrive after demolition, priced with no stated rate |
| "We handle permits" — with no permit line and no named party | Nobody has confirmed who pulls or pays for what |
| "We stand behind our work" — with no duration | The warranty has no length, so it cannot be enforced as a term |
| "Payment is due when we start, then 50%, then the rest at roughly halfway" | Front-loaded schedule: money leaves before meaningful work exists |
| "We'll fill in those blanks later" | The blanks are where price, dates, and cancellation rights live |
| Answers given verbally but not repeated in the document | By the time it matters, only the page counts |

A short answer is not itself a red flag. An answer that never reaches the contract is.

## 3. Put every answer where it can be enforced

> A verbal answer to a contract question is not an answer. It is a memory — and memories disagree about what was said.

Map each answer to its place in the document:

- **Scope and exclusions** → contract body plus a written scope attachment.
- **Allowances** → a list with amounts and assumptions.
- **Payment schedule** → milestone descriptions with amounts and dates.
- **Change orders** → the terms section: written, priced, signed before work proceeds, with stated rates.
- **Warranty** → its own clause with durations for labor and manufacturer coverage.
- **Cancellation** → a clause stating how to cancel and your cooling-off period. The FTC's cooling-off rule gives three business days to cancel when the contract is signed at a location other than the contractor's permanent place of business.

The FTC's contract checklist is a useful last pass before signing: written estimate, written contract, payment terms you can point to, and cancellation rights stated in the document. Ask the ten questions, then insist each answer appears in the contract before anyone signs anything. The negotiation is not the meeting — it is the document that comes out of it, which you can compare across bids with [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/).
