**A contractor may ask for a deposit limited by your state's law — California caps most jobs at $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less, Nevada uses the same formula, and Maine and Pennsylvania cap at one-third — and where no cap is verified here, the working rule is the Federal Trade Commission's: never pay the full amount before work starts, and never make the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied with it.**

A deposit buys the contractor's commitment to your schedule: materials ordered, crew booked, start date held. It is not a financing arrangement, and the moment it becomes one — half up front, the balance before demolition — your money is at risk on a job that does not exist yet.

## What counts as the deposit

Money moves in three places, and only the first is bounded by a deposit limit:

- **Deposit** — paid at or before signing, stated as a dollar figure, checked against your state's cap.
- **Progress payments** — each tied to a described milestone, with the amount named.
- **Final payment** — held until the work is complete and you are satisfied with it.

A cap reaches the first line only. Beyond it, the Federal Trade Commission's guidance applies everywhere: do not pay the full price before work starts, and do not make the final payment until the work is done. The warning signals are collected in [red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/), and [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/) shows where each payment term sits in the document.

## States with a verified deposit limit

Every row below was checked against the agency or statute named in the source. Nothing here is an estimate, and states we could not verify are deliberately absent.

| State | Deposit limit | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is less, for home improvement and pool work | California Attorney General; Contractors State License Board |
| Nevada | $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is less | Nevada State Contractors Board |
| Maine | No more than 1/3 of the total contract price, on contracts over $3,000 | Maine Attorney General |
| Pennsylvania | One-third of the contract price, or one-third plus the cost of special-order materials named in the contract | Pennsylvania Attorney General |

Two details matter. California's limit applies unless a valid blanket performance and payment bond is on file with the state licensing board, and the state's consumer guide says there are no exceptions for special order materials. Nevada's board says a down payment may exceed the cap only if the contractor has posted a $100,000 consumer protection bond with the board or was previously granted relief from posting a license bond. Maine allows the one-third limit to be waived only when you are told about the right and agree in writing, on a separate signed page. Pennsylvania's Attorney General describes the limit as applying to projects over $5,000.

## What a cap does and does not cover

A cap limits the down payment — money paid before or when the contract is signed — and does not by itself control what happens afterward. California's board, for example, also restricts progress payments so they cannot exceed the value of work completed and materials delivered, which is a separate protection from the deposit limit itself.

> The cap is a floor-level protection, not a target. A deposit inside the legal limit can still be larger than the work performed so far.

## Requests that deserve a pause

Some asks deserve verification before any money moves: the full contract price before work begins, cash only, a down payment above your state's limit, or payments that get ahead of the work completed on site. The FTC lists paying everything up front or dealing only in cash among the tactics scammers use, and a deposit sized by anxiety rather than by work is the same pattern stated quietly. Ask for the schedule in writing first.

## How to check the limit where you live

We verify only what a state agency or statute states in its own words, and we do not publish numbers we could not confirm. To find your own limit, go to the source: your state's contractor licensing board, your state attorney general's consumer protection division, or your state or local consumer agency. Ask three questions: is there a numeric cap, does it apply to home improvement of this size, and does the cap include material purchases?

While you are there, confirm the license number on the quote yourself using [how to verify a contractor license](/guides/how-to-verify-a-contractor-license/), and check the whole payment section with the field list in the [contractor quote template](/guides/contractor-quote-template/). The questions worth asking about money before signature are in [questions to ask before signing a contractor contract](/guides/questions-to-ask-before-signing-a-contractor-contract/).

## The bottom line

A deposit is legitimate, bounded, and small enough that losing it would not end your project: one small amount at signing, milestone payments as work is completed, and nothing final until the job is done and you are satisfied with it. If a request exceeds your state's limit or puts most of the contract price in the contractor's hands before any work exists, stop and verify the number before paying anything — contact your state or local consumer agency to confirm the limit that applies where you live.
