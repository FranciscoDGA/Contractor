**In residential remodeling, material markups are commonly published in the 10–30% range over contractor cost, and a markup above roughly 40% with no itemized breakdown is worth challenging — every range on this page is a published estimate, not a universal rule.**

Markup is the amount a contractor adds on top of what the material actually cost them. It is a normal part of contractor pricing, and it is almost never shown unless you ask. Here is the math, the published ranges, and the questions that turn a hidden number into a visible one.

## 1. Markup and margin are two different numbers

Markup is calculated on **cost**. Margin is calculated on **price**. Contractors and homeowners confuse them constantly, and a "30% margin" quote is a different price than a "30% markup" quote.

- Markup % = (price − cost) ÷ cost × 100
- Margin % = (price − cost) ÷ price × 100

| Contractor's cost | Charge to you | Markup on cost | Margin on price |
|---|---|---|---|
| $4,000 | $4,400 | 10% | 9.1% |
| $4,000 | $4,600 | 15% | 13.0% |
| $4,000 | $5,200 | 30% | 23.1% |
| $4,000 | $5,600 | 40% | 28.6% |

> Ask one clarifying question before you compare two bids: "Is that percentage a markup or a margin?" The same word describes two different prices.

Once you can run the calculation, you can check any materials line against the contractor's stated cost — which means the next question is what a defensible cost looks like.

## 2. What the published guides actually say

There is no single industry standard, and the published sources do not agree with each other:

- **Angi's** general contractor markup guide reports material markup typically around 7.5%–10%, which can reach 20%, and general contractor markup of 15%–20%.
- **Method's** markup guide puts material markup in a 7%–20% range and general contractor markup at 15%–20%.

Read those together and a pattern appears: materials-only figures sit lower, and the wider 10–30% band you often see quoted reflects markups that also carry overhead, delivery, and profit. Both are published estimates. Neither is a rule that any contractor is required to follow, and neither tells you whether your specific quote is fair.

Where markup starts to deserve pushback is the far end: a materials charge that implies **more than roughly 40% over cost, with no breakdown offered**, is worth challenging — not because a number above a line is automatically wrong, but because a charge that high and that opaque leaves you unable to verify anything else on the estimate.

## 3. Why the markup exists at all

A markup is not pure profit sitting in one bucket. The work behind buying materials for your project includes:

- **Sourcing.** Trade accounts, supplier relationships, and knowing which distributor actually stocks the specified product.
- **Delivery and handling.** Freight, truck time, fuel, unloading, and staging material on site.
- **Returns and damage.** Restocking fees, a cracked box, a short shipment, an over-ordered batch. The contractor absorbs these, not you.
- **Warranty administration.** If a fixture fails or a box of tile comes up short, someone coordinates the replacement and covers the labor to redo the work, not just the part.
- **Price movement.** Your quote may be held for 30 or 60 days while supplier prices move underneath it.

That list is the honest case for a markup, and it is also the list you can test a quote against: a contractor who can name those costs has a basis for the number.

## 4. What to ask for

Ask in this order, and get the answers on paper:

1. **Cost and markup as separate numbers** on every materials line above about $2,000.
2. **The stated markup percentage**, written into the contract or the estimate, so it applies to the base quote.
3. **The same percentage applied to change-order materials** — a different rate on changes is a second pricing scheme.
4. **Supplier paperwork.** An invoice or order summary works; asking for it with supplier details blacked out is reasonable.
5. **Unit pricing and quantity** where a lump sum was quoted instead — the alternative is the bundled-line problem in [how to read a contractor estimate](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/).
6. **Whether tax, delivery, and disposal are inside the materials figure** or billed as separate lines.

Asking for a percentage is a normal request. How a contractor responds tells you whether that number was ever going to be on the page.

## 5. How to spot a double-dip

A double-dip is the same dollar of material earning markup twice. Check for these patterns:

| Double-dip | What it looks like | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Retail price plus markup | Materials billed at the store's sticker price, then marked up again | Is this your cost or retail? |
| Delivery charged twice | A delivery fee line, plus delivery inside the marked-up materials line | Which line covers delivery? |
| Markup on subcontractor materials | The general contractor marks up the sub's labor and the sub's materials separately | What markup applies to subcontractor quotes? |
| Allowance plus overage markup | A low allowance, then a marked-up overage billed as a change order | What rate applies to allowance overages? |
| Tax buried in the lump sum | "Materials: $8,400" with no tax line | Is tax included, and in what amount? |

The last check is consistency: one percentage should apply to the base quote, to allowances, and to change orders. Three different numbers mean three pricing schemes for one project.

## The bottom line

A fair markup is a disclosed markup. Get cost and markup as separate numbers, apply one percentage across the base quote and every change order, and measure the result against published ranges rather than against a rule.
