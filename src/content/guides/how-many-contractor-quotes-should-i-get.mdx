**Get three written quotes for the same scope on any job over a few thousand dollars — two is enough for simple, standardized work, and five usually wastes your time and the contractors'.**

That baseline matches consumer guidance: the Federal Trade Commission's home repair material tells homeowners to get multiple written estimates before hiring, and its advice on choosing is explicit — do not automatically choose the lowest bidder, and ask for an explanation when the estimates differ a lot. The condition attached to the number matters more than the number itself. Three quotes on three different scopes produce three meaningless figures.

## Why the spread matters

One quote gives you no reference point at all. Two quotes give you a spread but no pattern: you cannot tell whether the low number or the high number is the odd one out. Three quotes let you see whether a middle figure is the norm or an outlier.

The shape of the spread tells you what to fix next:

- **Quotes that land close together** usually mean the scope is well defined and priced consistently in your market. You can start weighing terms instead of price.
- **One quote far below the others** usually means it excludes work the others include, carries thin allowances, or assumes site conditions nobody has checked yet.
- **One quote far above the others** can mean a fuller inclusion list, a heavier scope, or simply a contractor who is not chasing the job.

A wide spread is not a reason to average the numbers or to grab the middle. It is a reason to find out which bid describes the job you actually want.

## When two quotes is enough

Two written quotes hold up when most of these are true:

- The job is small and the scope is unambiguous — a like-for-like replacement, a single-room repaint, one appliance install.
- The work is standardized, so pricing in your area does not vary much from provider to provider.
- You already know local rates well enough to judge either number on its own.
- The two numbers land close together and the terms match.

When two quotes disagree sharply, that is the signal to collect a third rather than to split the difference. A third number breaks the tie and exposes which of the first two was guessing.

## When five quotes wastes time

Past three or four, the returns drop. Each additional bid costs you walkthroughs, explanations, and scheduling, and it costs each contractor a visit they could spend on paying work. By the fifth visit you are usually re-explaining the same scope to someone who will not price it differently from the third.

There is a practical cost too: contractors who see a homeowner collecting a long list of bids will either price for that competition or decline the walkthrough altogether. Small outfits often skip jobs they expect to go to whoever bids lowest.

| Situation | Quotes to get | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Simple job under a few thousand dollars | 2 | Scope is easy to verify; the spread tells you enough |
| Renovation or multi-trade work | 3 | Establishes a middle number and exposes thin scopes |
| Quotes disagree by more than 30% | 3, on one written scope | The disagreement is a scope problem before it is a price problem |
| Few providers in your area | 2–3 | The market may not support five competing bids |

## Normalize the scope before you count bids

Three quotes only work if they quote the same job. Before you collect them, write one short scope document: what is being done and where, the materials or grade of materials, what gets demolished and hauled away, what happens after the trades leave (paint, trim, cleanup), who handles permits, and your target start and finish.

Send that identical document to every contractor. Ask each one to price it line by line, list exclusions, and flag any assumption they read differently from your wording. Anything a contractor adds or omits should show up as a written change to your scope, not as a silent difference in the total.

> When bids differ by 30% or more, the difference is almost always scope, not price. Re-price against one written scope before you treat any number as real.

Once the numbers are normalized, run them through the [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) and read the [line-by-line estimate method](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/) for what each section should contain.

## The FTC's rule: get multiple estimates, don't auto-pick the lowest

The FTC's guidance on home improvement comes down to two instructions:

1. **Get multiple estimates.** A written estimate should describe the work to be done, the materials, the completion date, and the price.
2. **Do not treat the lowest number as the automatic winner.** Weigh price against the written scope, the contract terms, and the contractor's license and insurance status.

The FTC also warns about estimates that arrive with a hard deadline attached — pressure for an immediate decision is a documented tactic, not a sign of a busy schedule.

Your job at the end of the process is not to find the cheapest number. It is to identify which number buys the job you described, on terms you can live with.

## Choosing between three good quotes

Once scope is aligned, compare in this order:

- **Exclusions** — who leaves out permits, disposal, or finish work.
- **Allowances** — whether placeholder numbers match the selections you have in mind.
- **Payment schedule** — milestone triggers versus calendar dates, and deposit size.
- **Warranty and timeline** — stated durations, and what counts as completion.
- **Total** — last, never first.

The full side-by-side method is in [how to compare contractor bids](/guides/how-to-compare-contractor-bids/), and the difference between a loose estimate and a fixed offer is covered in [estimate vs. quote vs. bid](/guides/contractor-estimate-vs-quote-vs-bid/).

## The bottom line

Three written quotes on one written scope is the working baseline for jobs over a few thousand dollars. Two is enough when the job is simple and the numbers agree; a fourth or fifth bid rarely changes the answer. Spend your effort normalizing scope, not multiplying bids.
