**Normalize scope first, compare the bids line by line, and read the totals last — a comparison that starts with the bottom number tells you nothing about what you are buying.**

Bids are only comparable when they describe the same job. The method below turns three proposals into one spreadsheet you can defend, and it works whether the totals land within a few hundred dollars of each other or differ by half again.

## Step 1: Put one scope in front of every bid

Write the scope yourself, in plain language: rooms or systems involved, materials or material grade, demo and disposal, finish work after the trades, permits, cleanup, and your target dates. Send that same document to every bidder and ask for a line-by-line response.

If a contractor priced the job from a walkthrough without your written scope, ask them to re-quote against it. Their number may move, and that movement is information — it tells you which assumptions were baked into the original figure.

> Bids that differ by 30% or more are almost never a pricing disagreement. They are two or three different projects wearing the same label.

## Step 2: Build the comparison table

One row per comparison point, one column per contractor. Keep it on a single page so nothing hides in a footnote.

| Row | What to line up | The question that settles it |
|---|---|---|
| Exclusions | Permits, demo, disposal, paint, repairs after trades, moving appliances | What does each bid leave out that the others include? |
| Allowances | Amount and what it assumes for tile, fixtures, counters, cabinets | Would my actual selections fit inside that allowance? |
| Payment schedule | Deposit size, milestone triggers, final payment conditions | Does money move with completed work or with the calendar? |
| Warranty and timeline | Labor warranty duration, manufacturer warranty, start and completion dates | Are durations stated, and what counts as completion? |

Fill the table with the words each bid actually uses. Where a cell is empty, that is a question, not a zero.

## Step 3: Read the rows in order

**Exclusions first.** The cheapest bid usually excludes the most. When one bid omits something the others include, ask that contractor whether it is excluded, forgotten, or priced elsewhere — and get the answer in writing. That single question surfaces most of the real gap between competing bids.

**Allowances second.** An allowance is a placeholder, not a price. Compare the amount and the assumption together: a higher allowance on the same material is a higher expected cost, not generosity.

**Payment schedule third.** Line up the milestones. A schedule that ties payments to completed work protects your money on both sides; a schedule driven by dates pays for progress that may not exist yet. The FTC's guidance is direct: never pay the full amount up front, and hold the final payment until the work is done and you are satisfied with it.

**Warranty and timeline fourth.** Durations and definitions side by side. A labor warranty with a stated length beats an implied promise; a completion date with a definition of "completion" beats a hopeful range.

**Totals last.** Only now does the bottom number mean something, because everything above it lines up.

## When bids differ by 30% or more

A gap that wide has a small number of causes, and they are findable:

1. **Different scopes.** One bid includes work another excludes. Re-price both against your written scope.
2. **Different assumptions.** Site conditions, prep work, or material grade were read differently. Ask each contractor to state assumptions in writing.
3. **Different business models.** One firm carries more overhead, one is booking further out, one is hungry for work. Legitimate variation — but you are choosing terms, not just a number.
4. **One bid is a placeholder.** A number produced to win the walkthrough gets refined later through change orders.

Work through them in that order. If the gap survives a scope normalization, it is a real price difference and you can weigh it against terms with a clear head. The [quote fairness tool](/tools/quote-fairness/) gives you a published-range reference once your numbers describe the same job.

## Turning the table into a decision

Rank the bids on the rows that matter most to you, not on total. A bid that wins on exclusions, allowances, payment schedule, and warranty is often not the cheapest line in the spreadsheet — and it is usually the one with fewer ways to grow.

Then negotiate. Our [negotiation guide](/guides/should-you-negotiate-with-a-contractor/) covers what to ask for after the comparison, and [red flags in a contractor quote](/guides/red-flags-in-a-contractor-quote/) covers the terms that should send you back to step one. If two bids still look identical on paper, [reading an estimate line by line](/guides/how-to-read-a-contractor-estimate/) shows where the last differences hide.

## The bottom line

Scope, then line by line, then totals — in that order, every time. Build the four-row table, resolve every omission in writing, and a 30% gap usually collapses into a scope question you can answer in an afternoon.
